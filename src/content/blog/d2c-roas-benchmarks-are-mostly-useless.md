Everyone Googles the same thing before they scale: what's a good ROAS. And the internet hands back a number, usually somewhere between 2x and 4x, as if that number means anything on its own.

It mostly doesn't. Here's the state of the published benchmarks, why they don't travel, and what to track instead.

## The numbers everyone quotes

The most-cited figure is the average ecommerce ROAS of about 2.87x, which comes from Triple Whale's 2025 data on thousands of D2C stores. Google's own Economic Impact report is more conservative, at roughly 2:1.

The platform spread is wide. Published benchmark reports put median Google Ads ROAS around 3.5x and Meta around 1.9x to 2.2x, with Meta retargeting closer to 3.6x. That retargeting number makes sense: you're showing ads to people who already visited.

Then there's the category spread, and this is where it gets interesting. Beauty and personal care on Meta comes in around 1.6x. Fashion sits near 2.6x. Baby products run hot at roughly 4.4x. Same platform, same year, a near fourfold spread between categories.

If you're a beauty brand reading "good ROAS is 4x," you'd shut off a perfectly healthy account. If you sell baby products and treat 2x as success, you're leaving money on the table.

## Why the numbers don't travel

First, margin. A 2x ROAS on a 70% margin product is a different business from a 2x on a 25% margin product. Benchmarks report revenue divided by spend. They say nothing about what survived to the bank. The beauty brand at 1.6x with 70% margins is often more profitable than the electronics brand at 3x with 15% margins.

Second, order value and repeat purchase. Most benchmarks count the first purchase only. A brand with a 900 rupee AOV and no repeat is a different business from one with a 2,500 rupee AOV and a subscription. Blended ROAS over 90 days can be double what a first-purchase benchmark suggests.

Third, attribution. Meta's reported ROAS, Triple Whale's, and your own backend rarely agree, because they count different windows and different touchpoints. A benchmark from a tool you don't use is a number from a different reality.

## What benchmarks are actually good for

Direction, not targets. Use them to answer one question: is my account in the right ballpark, or is something broken? If your Meta ROAS is 0.4x and the median for your category is 2x, something is wrong and probably fixable. If you're at 1.9x and the median is 2.1x, that's noise, not a problem.

They're also useful for sanity-checking your channel mix. If your category does better on Google than Meta on average, that's a reason to look at your split, not a reason to panic.

## What we actually see

Across the D2C accounts we run in India, the pattern matches the published data but shifts hard by category and offer. Beauty and personal care accounts rarely clear 2x on cold Meta traffic at scale, and that's fine because the margins and repeat purchase support it. Fashion runs higher on Google Shopping than Meta, mostly because search captures buying intent. Food and FMCG accounts live on volume and repeat purchase, so first-order ROAS looks weak while the 90-day blended number is what actually matters.

The accounts that scale are not the ones with the highest ROAS. They're the ones tracking contribution margin per order and marginal ROAS on the last rupee of spend, which is the point in our earlier post on why [your ROAS number is lying to you](/blog/your-roas-number-is-lying-to-you).

## The short version

Stop treating a published average as a target. Find your category's range, confirm you're in it, then ignore the benchmark and manage to margin. If someone quotes you a single good ROAS number without asking your category, your margins, or your repeat purchase rate, they're selling something.
